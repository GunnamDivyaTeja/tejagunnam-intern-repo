# Inclusive Design Reflection

## Research and Learn

### Who are considered vulnerable populations, and what challenges do they face in digital spaces?

Vulnerable populations are groups of people who are more likely to be harmed or left out if a product is not designed with care. Some examples are:

- Neurodivergent people, such as people with ADHD or autism
- People with disabilities (vision, hearing, movement)
- People with mental health conditions
- Children and older people
- People with low digital skills or limited English

Challenges they might face in digital spaces:

- Cluttered screens and too many choices, which can be overwhelming
- Notifications, animations and sounds that distract or cause sensory overload
- Unclear instructions or wording that can be read in more than one way
- Designs that push people to do things they did not plan to do
- Sharing sensitive data, like health details, without fully understanding how it will be used
- Feeling judged or guilty when they fall behind

### Ethical considerations when designing for or working with neurodivergent people

- Do not overwhelm the user. Keep screens simple and show one thing at a time.
- Respect sensory needs. Avoid flashing content and loud sounds, and let users turn off animations and sounds.
- Communicate clearly. Say exactly what will happen, with no hidden steps.
- Do not use guilt or shame to motivate people. A missed habit should not feel like a failure.
- Give the user control over reminders, blocking and settings.
- Treat health related data, like an ADHD diagnosis, with extra care and only use it with consent.
- Do not make assumptions. Every person with ADHD or autism is different.
- Include neurodivergent people in testing and feedback.

### Making interactions and content accessible for people with ADHD or autism

- Use simple and direct language, with short sentences
- Break tasks into small steps
- Keep navigation the same on every screen so it is predictable
- Reduce cognitive load by showing only what is needed right now
- Use headings, bullet points and visuals instead of long paragraphs
- Give clear feedback after each action, so the user knows it worked
- Allow people to pause, snooze or come back later without losing progress
- Avoid idioms, sarcasm and vague words like "soon" or "a few"

### Supporting neurodivergent team members

- Be clear and specific in messages, and say what is needed and by when
- Put important things in writing, not only in calls
- Share the agenda before a meeting, and send notes after it
- Respect different working styles, like camera off, async replies, or extra time to think
- Be flexible with working hours where possible
- Do not read tone into short or very direct messages
- Ask people how they like to work, and read their user manuals

## Reflection

### Adjusting my communication style

- Write short and clear messages with one main point
- Say exactly what I need and by when, instead of hinting
- Avoid sarcasm, idioms and unclear jokes in work messages
- Use lists and steps instead of long paragraphs
- Give people time to respond, and not expect an instant reply
- Ask if I am not sure what someone meant, instead of guessing
- Be patient and kind when someone asks the same question again

### Common UX or communication pitfalls

- Too many notifications or popups at once
- Long onboarding with a lot of text and many settings up front
- Streaks and leaderboards that make people feel bad when they break a streak
- Wording that sounds like blame, such as "You failed to complete your habit"
- Blocking apps or websites without clearly explaining why, or without an easy way out for something urgent
- Changing the layout or flow often, which breaks routines
- Error messages that do not say what to do next
- In a team, giving vague tasks or only giving instructions verbally

### One practical change I can make in my work

As an ML engineer, I will think about how model outputs feel to the user, not only how accurate they are. For example, if a model decides to block a website or writes a motivational message, I will check that the wording is clear, kind and free of blame, and that the user can understand why it happened and can override it.

## Task

### First-person account I read

I read [Understanding and Improving Productivity as a Product Manager with ADHD](https://adhdpm.substack.com/p/67-understanding-and-improving-productivity) by Rawi Nanakul, who has ADHD and works as a product manager and ADHD coach.

What I learned from it:

- He tried 4 productivity apps and 3 AI tools in one month. Most of them were opened for 5 minutes and never used again. He calls it a graveyard of unused apps on his phone.
- He used to believe that the right app would fix his focus problems. What he found is that a tool only helps when it fits how his brain already works, and it cannot force him to focus.
- His advice is to focus on routines over results, and to remember that the person is not the problem.

For Focus Bear, this tells me that the first few minutes in the app matter a lot. If the setup feels like too much work, a user with ADHD may never come back. It also shows that the app should support the user's own routine and not make them feel like they are the problem.

### One design or communication improvement for Focus Bear

When a user misses a habit or breaks a streak, the app could show a supportive message with one small next step, instead of only showing the broken streak. For example: "No worries, today was a busy one. Want to start with just 2 minutes?" This keeps the user from feeling like they failed and makes it easier to start again.

### A supportive response to a user struggling with executive functioning

**User:** I keep setting up my morning routine in Focus Bear but I never follow it. I skip it every day and now I feel useless. Maybe this app is not for me.

**My response:**

Hi, thank you for telling us about this. Please do not be hard on yourself. A lot of people find it difficult to stick to a routine at first, and it does not mean you are doing something wrong.

Here are a few things that might help:

1. Make the routine smaller. Try keeping only 1 or 2 habits for now, and remove the rest.
2. Make each habit short. Even 2 minutes is fine to start with.
3. Pick the easiest habit first, so you get a quick win in the morning.

You can always add more habits later once this feels comfortable. There is no rush.

If you would like, tell me what your current routine looks like and I can help you simplify it. We are happy to help.
